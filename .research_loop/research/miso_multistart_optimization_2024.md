## 1. Key Contribution — What Is MISO?

MISO (Multiple Initial Solutions for Optimization) addresses a fundamental problem in nonlinear optimization under time constraints: most iterative solvers are highly sensitive to initialization, and a single bad starting point leads to convergence at a poor local optimum. The standard practice of using a single warm-start or default initialization leaves significant performance on the table.

The core insight is that optimization landscapes are multimodal — multiple regions of attraction exist, and the best solution depends on the specific problem instance. A learning system trained to predict a single initialization inevitably collapses to a mean estimate, which often corresponds to a local optimum between two good solutions. MISO instead trains a single neural network to predict K diverse initial solutions simultaneously, then either selects the most promising one before optimization (single-optimizer mode) or runs K optimizers in parallel and picks the best final result (multiple-optimizer mode).

The theoretical guarantee is notable: by ensuring the default warm-start is always among the K candidates, MISO guarantees final performance at least as good as the baseline warm-start. In practice it substantially beats this lower bound.

## 2. Technical Approach — How Does MISO Generate Multiple Initial Solutions?

### Network Architecture

A standard Transformer encoder produces K candidate initial solutions from a single forward pass:
- 4 layers, 2 attention heads, 64-dimensional embeddings
- Input: concatenated warm-start trajectory error + problem parameters ψ
- Output: K candidate initializations {x̂ᵏ_init}ᵏ₌₁ᴷ
- Inference time: ~0.6–0.8 ms regardless of K (constant overhead)

### Training: Three Diversity-Promoting Losses

The central challenge in training a multi-output model is mode collapse — all K outputs converging to the same solution. MISO uses three loss functions to enforce diversity:

**Pairwise Distance Loss (L_PD):** Combines a regression term penalizing distance to ground-truth optimal solutions with an explicit diversity penalty between output pairs. A hyperparameter αₖ balances accuracy vs. dispersion.

**Winner-Takes-All Loss (L_WTA):** Only the single closest output to the ground-truth receives a gradient update: `min_k { L_reg(x̂ᵏ_init, x*) }`. Non-winning outputs are free to explore other regions of the space without being pulled toward the known optimum. This is hyperparameter-free and naturally promotes diversity.

**Mixture Loss (L_MIX):** Combines both: `min_k { L_reg(...) + αₖ Φ(L_PD,k(...)) }` where Φ is a bounded function (min or tanh) that caps the dispersion contribution, preventing runaway diversity at the cost of solution quality.

### Training Data

- 500,000 problem instances per task
- Oracle solutions generated by extended optimizer runs (10 iterations vs. 2 online; 50 samples vs. 3)
- 125 training epochs, batch size 1024, learning rates 0.0001–0.001

### Deployment Modes

**Single-optimizer:** A learned or heuristic selection function Λ picks the most promising of the K candidates, then a single optimizer runs from that point. Keeps compute cost identical to single-start.

**Multiple-optimizer:** All K candidates are used to initialize K parallel optimizer instances. The best final result is selected. This is more expensive but extracts maximum benefit from diverse initializations.

## 3. Results — How Much Does Multi-Start Improve Over Single-Start?

Results are compared against (a) naive warm-start, (b) single-output regression, (c) ensemble of K separate networks, and (d) perturbation methods (adding Gaussian noise to warm-start).

### Cart-Pole (Box-DDP optimizer)
| Method | Sequential Cost |
|--------|----------------|
| Warm-start (baseline) | 11.69 ± 0.84 |
| Single-output regression | 6.18 ± 0.47 |
| MISO Winner-Takes-All | **0.83 ± 0.06** |
| MISO Mix | **0.79 ± 0.04** |
| Oracle upper bound | 4.54 ± 0.71 |

MISO achieves **14× improvement over warm-start** and notably **beats the oracle** upper bound computed by the authors — this occurs because the oracle uses a single initialization while MISO with multiple-optimizers explores multiple basins.

### Reacher Task (MPPI sampling-based optimizer)
| Method | Sequential Cost |
|--------|----------------|
| Warm-start | 13.48 ± 0.88 |
| Single-output regression | 19.56 ± 0.52 |
| MISO Winner-Takes-All | **2.72 ± 0.21** |
| MISO Mix | **2.44 ± 0.20** |

**5× improvement over warm-start.** Note that single-output regression degrades vs. warm-start here — averaging collapses to a bad region.

### Autonomous Driving (iLQR trajectory optimizer)
| Method | Sequential Cost |
|--------|----------------|
| Warm-start | 283.86 ± 37.91 |
| Ensemble (32 separate models) | 52.59 ± 4.81 |
| MISO Winner-Takes-All | **30.75 ± 2.15** |
| MISO Mix | 33.38 ± 2.21 |

**9× improvement over warm-start, 1.7× improvement over a 32-model ensemble** — with constant inference overhead vs. the ensemble's linear scaling.

### Scaling with K

Performance improves consistently as K increases, while inference cost stays constant (single network forward pass). This is a key advantage over ensemble methods, which require K separate model evaluations.

## 4. Relevance to Trajectory Optimization with L-BFGS

Our trajectory optimizer in `planning/trajectory_optimizer.py` uses L-BFGS (via `scipy.optimize.minimize`) for min-snap polynomial trajectories. The optimization landscape for time-optimal trajectories is highly nonlinear and multimodal — different segment time allocations, different corridor assignments, and different polynomial initialization points lead to very different local optima.

### Where Multimodality Hurts Us Now

1. **Segment time allocation:** The time allocation τ = [τ₁, τ₂, ..., τₙ] for N gate segments has many local optima. Fast inner segments may require different time budgets than outer arcs. A single initial guess (e.g., uniform allocation) often settles in a suboptimal basin.

2. **Helix and chicane geometries:** The Strait of Hormuz course has tight turns and a helix. The optimizer must choose which side of the feasible corridor to track; starting in the wrong basin produces suboptimal curves through these sections.

3. **L-BFGS sensitivity:** L-BFGS with line search is sensitive to the curvature landscape near initialization. For steeply nonlinear problems (racing trajectories with dynamic feasibility constraints), the initial Hessian approximation critically determines which local minimum is found.

4. **Race time vs. tracking error tradeoff:** The current tension between race time (13.31s after iter 46) and gate-4 tracking error (−5.0% remaining) suggests the optimizer is in a basin that represents a locally Pareto-optimal tradeoff, but a different initialization could reveal a globally better solution.

### How MISO's Insights Apply

MISO provides two applicable insights regardless of whether ML is used:

**Insight 1: Multi-start beats single-start dramatically in multimodal landscapes.** The 5–14× improvements observed in MISO experiments suggest that our current single-start L-BFGS is almost certainly not finding the global optimum. Even simple random restarts should yield measurable improvement.

**Insight 2: Diversity in initialization matters more than quantity of restarts.** MISO's diversity losses are designed to prevent redundant initializations from clustering together. The equivalent for heuristic multi-start is to sample from structurally different families of initial guesses (not just perturbed versions of the same guess).

## 5. Actionable Takeaways — Multi-Start L-BFGS Without ML

The MISO framework requires training data and a neural network. For our use case, simpler heuristic multi-start achieves most of the benefit. Here is a concrete implementation strategy:

### Strategy A: Structured Diverse Initializations

Generate K candidate initial time allocations from structurally different heuristics:

1. **Uniform allocation:** τᵢ = total_time / N for all segments (current default)
2. **Distance-proportional:** τᵢ ∝ |gate[i+1] - gate[i]| (longer arcs get more time)
3. **Curvature-weighted:** τᵢ ∝ curvature[i]^0.5 (tight turns get more time)
4. **Speed-profile-derived:** τᵢ derived from a simplified point-mass speed profile on the racing line
5. **Perturbed baseline:** Random ±20% perturbations of the best-known solution from previous iterations
6. **Aggressive (fast):** All τᵢ set 20% below the distance-proportional estimate

Run L-BFGS from each, take the best result. With K=6 and L-BFGS converging in ~50 iterations each, the total compute is 6× the current cost but should find substantially better solutions.

### Strategy B: Sequential Refinement (Warm Restart from Best)

Run the structured initializations above but use the best result from each as the warm-start for the next. This is a greedy variant that often converges faster than parallel multi-start.

### Strategy C: Randomized Multi-Start with Clipping

Add noise to the current best solution and re-optimize, keeping the best result:

```python
results = [current_solution]
for _ in range(K):
    perturbed = current_tau * (1 + 0.15 * np.random.randn(N))
    perturbed = np.clip(perturbed, tau_min, tau_max)
    result = scipy.optimize.minimize(objective, perturbed, method='L-BFGS-B', ...)
    results.append(result)
best = min(results, key=lambda r: r.fun)
```

### Strategy D: Segment-Wise Perturbation (Most Targeted)

For specific problematic gates (e.g., gate-4 which has −5.0% error), perturb only the time allocation for that segment and its neighbors while holding others fixed. This is a low-cost targeted multi-start for known problem areas.

### Implementation Notes

- The L-BFGS `maxiter` can be reduced for early-stage multi-start candidates (use 30 iters to screen, then full 200 iters on the top 2 survivors)
- Save the multi-start results across race laps as a warm-start cache — the best initialization found in the offline phase seeds the online optimizer
- The MISO guarantee applies: always include the current default as one of the K candidates, so multi-start cannot regress vs. baseline

### Expected Gain

Based on MISO's results on similar nonlinear trajectory problems (iLQR autonomous driving showed 9× improvement), even simple 4-start heuristic multi-start should reduce race time or tracking error by 5–20% for the same number of optimization iterations. The gain is largest when the current solution has high gate-specific error variance (as we see with gate-4).

## Summary Assessment

MISO is a mature and practically well-grounded paper. The key theoretical insight — that initialization diversity is more valuable than initialization accuracy in multimodal landscapes — is directly applicable to our trajectory optimization problem. The 5–15× improvement figures are credible and consistent across three different optimizer types (DDP, MPPI, iLQR), suggesting the benefit is general.

For the AI Grand Prix project, the most actionable near-term step is implementing heuristic multi-start L-BFGS with K=4–6 structurally diverse initializations. This requires no ML, no additional dependencies, and can be integrated into `planning/trajectory_optimizer.py` in the offline precomputation phase. Given our current gate-4 error (−5.0% remaining after iter 46) and the general sensitivity of our L-BFGS to segment time allocation, this is a high-probability improvement path.

The full MISO framework (with neural network and diversity training) is worth considering if we reach the point of needing real-time trajectory re-planning during the race, since it achieves K-diverse solutions at the cost of a single forward pass.
