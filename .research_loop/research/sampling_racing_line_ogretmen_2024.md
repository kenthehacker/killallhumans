# Sampling-Based Motion Planning with Online Racing Line Generation
- **URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18643
- **Authors**: Levent Ögretmen, Matthias Rowold, Alexander Langmann, Boris Lohmann (Technical University of Munich)
- **Year**: 2024
- **Venue**: 2024 IEEE Intelligent Vehicles Symposium (IV), Jeju Island, Korea, pp. 811–818. DOI: 10.1109/IV58885.2024.10588726

---

## Key Contribution

This paper presents a sampling-based local trajectory planning algorithm for autonomous racing that addresses two fundamental shortcomings of prior approaches. First, existing sampling-based planners use jerk-optimal polynomial edges whose simplified geometry fails to track the true racing line on complex circuits — they work on ovals but degrade on tight chicanes and combined-feature layouts. Second, prior methods operate exclusively in 2D, ignoring the three-dimensional structure of real race tracks (banking, elevation changes, grade), which alters effective grip via the apparent vertical load.

The paper's central contribution is online racing line generation: instead of committing to a single pre-computed offline racing line for the full lap, the algorithm continuously computes a time-optimal path from the current vehicle state over a limited spatial horizon. This short-horizon, receding-horizon racing line is then used as the reference for the sampling-based planner's cost function, replacing the static offline solution that quickly becomes stale when the vehicle deviates (e.g., in overtaking or after a disturbance). The result is a planner that maintains lap-time parity with the offline racing line even on complex 3D circuits, and that can significantly reduce lap times relative to prior work in multi-agent scenarios.

## Technical Approach

### 1. Framework: Sampling-Based Planning in a Frenet Frame

The planner operates in the Frenet-Serret coordinate system — tangent (s), normal (d), and bi-normal directions aligned to the track center line. This frame decouples longitudinal progress (velocity profile) from lateral position (raceline offset), making it natural to separately optimize each. A large ensemble of candidate trajectories is generated by sampling over terminal states in this frame, each connected to the vehicle's current state by a jerk-optimal polynomial (5th-order for lateral, 4th-order for longitudinal) that satisfies endpoint boundary conditions on position, velocity, and acceleration.

Jerk-optimal polynomials minimize ∫(jerk²)dt over each segment given fixed endpoint boundary conditions. In closed form:

    d(t) = a₀ + a₁t + a₂t² + a₃t³ + a₄t⁴ + a₅t⁵

where coefficients aᵢ are solved uniquely from the six boundary conditions (d₀, ḋ₀, d̈₀, d_f, ḋ_f, d̈_f). This produces smooth candidate paths with bounded jerk that the low-level controller can track.

### 2. Online Racing Line Generation (the key innovation)

The offline minimum-lap-time (MLT) problem finds the time-optimal line for the full circuit under the vehicle's global dynamic constraints. This is solved once before the race using a point-mass model with a g-g-g-v envelope (see below). The result is a reference trajectory that is globally optimal — but it assumes the vehicle is always on that line, which fails in multi-vehicle scenarios or after disturbances.

Ögretmen et al. replace this global reference with an online, short-horizon racing line. At each planning step, they solve a truncated MLT problem from the current vehicle state over a spatial horizon (a fixed arc-length window ahead), yielding a local time-optimal line segment. This local segment is used as the reference in the cost function. Because it is re-solved from the current state, it naturally adapts to any offset from the global line without requiring explicit replanning triggers. The follow-on work (Langmann et al., IV 2025) extends this further by shifting the sampling domain from time to space (fixed spatial horizon S), which better captures apex geometry for track sections with large velocity variation.

### 3. The g-g-g-v Dynamic Constraint Model

To correctly account for 3D track effects, the paper uses a g-g-g-v (or gg-g-v) point-mass model. The standard 2D g-g diagram constrains lateral (ay) and longitudinal (ax) accelerations within an elliptical or diamond friction envelope. The 3D extension adds apparent vertical acceleration (az), which changes with track banking and grade. The allowed ay–ax envelope then becomes a function of the local az component:

    √(ay² + ax²) ≤ μ · (g + az)  (simplified form)

In practice the envelope is measured or estimated empirically as a 3D friction diamond parameterized by velocity v and vertical load. This means the planner correctly exploits the extra grip available on banked sections (positive az increases the friction budget) and is conservative on crests (negative az reduces it). Ignoring az leads to either unnecessarily slow trajectories on banked turns or infeasible commands near crests.

### 4. Cost Function

Candidate trajectories are evaluated by a multi-term cost functional (formulation from Frenetix framework and confirmed by the Langmann 2025 follow-on paper):

    J = w_lat · Δd²_raceline
      + w_curv · Δκ²_raceline
      + w_vel · Δv²_target
      + w_coll · risk_collision
      + w_acc · violations_accel
      + w_jerk · (jerk_lateral + jerk_longitudinal)

where:
- `Δd_raceline`: lateral deviation from the (online-generated) racing line
- `Δκ_raceline`: deviation in curvature from the racing line
- `Δv_target`: deviation from the racing line's velocity profile
- `risk_collision`: proximity and severity measure relative to other vehicles
- `violations_accel`: penalty for exceeding the g-g-g-v envelope
- jerk terms: smoothness regularization

Typical weights in the Frenetix implementation: `distance_to_reference_path = 3.0`, `lateral_jerk = 1.0`, `longitudinal_jerk = 1.0`, `velocity_offset = 0.1`, `distance_to_obstacles = 0.1`, with safety terms (`prediction`) weighted very heavily (~200) to prioritize feasibility.

The feasible trajectory with the lowest J is selected. The online racing line sets the reference for `Δd`, `Δκ`, and `Δv` terms simultaneously, which is what makes the approach work on complex tracks — rather than penalizing deviation from a globally fixed offline line, the reference is always locally optimal from the current state.

### 5. Sampling Strategy

Each planning cycle generates N trajectories by sampling terminal conditions over a grid in (s_f, d_f, ṡ_f) space. Temporal sampling (used in Ögretmen 2024) fixes the time horizon T and samples terminal positions at t = T; spatial sampling (introduced in Langmann 2025) fixes arc-length horizon S and samples terminal positions at s = s₀ + S. Spatial sampling better captures apex locations because a fixed time horizon at high speed reaches further down the track, potentially skipping tight corners.

The implementation is computationally efficient: the TUM Frenetix C++ library reports 8ms for 800 trajectories on typical hardware, giving >100 Hz planning frequency even with large trajectory sets.

## Results

The paper's simulation experiments demonstrate:

- **Lower lap times** on complex (non-oval) circuits compared to prior sampling-based planners that use a fixed offline racing line.
- **Improved utilization of dynamic limits**: the g-g-g-v-aware planner pushes closer to the friction boundary on banked sections while remaining feasible, whereas 2D planners either under-use grip on banked turns or become infeasible.
- **Multi-vehicle scenarios**: when an opponent forces a deviation from the racing line, the online racing line re-optimizes from the current (displaced) state, recovering time-optimal behavior faster than replanning from the global offline solution.

Quantitative lap-time figures are not publicly accessible (paper is paywalled), but the follow-on paper (Langmann 2025) reports that its spatial-sampling extension further reduces lap times relative to the Ögretmen 2024 temporal baseline on circuits with high-curvature sections, confirming the base method's competitiveness.

## Relevance to Our System

Our current `planning/racing_line.py` uses a multi-start L-BFGS optimizer with a proxy objective of `speed_weight * path_length + smoothness_weight * curvature²`. We already have N_STARTS=10 candidate racing lines; the open question is which of these 10 to actually use downstream. Our context states we want to replace the proxy selection criterion (lowest objective value) with actual kinematic sim tracking error.

This paper is directly relevant to that problem in the following ways:

**Candidate selection via simulation tracking error vs. proxy cost.** The Ögretmen 2024 cost functional explicitly includes `Δv_target` (velocity profile deviation) and curvature deviation terms alongside lateral offset — which are proxies for controller trackability. The key insight is that the best proxy for which candidate racing line is actually fastest is the one that produces minimum tracking error under the vehicle's real dynamics, not minimum path length + curvature². This is exactly what we want to implement: run each of our 10 L-BFGS candidates through a lightweight kinematic simulation, measure the integrated tracking error (equivalent to their `Δd_raceline` cost), and select the candidate with minimum tracking error rather than minimum geometric proxy.

**The online racing line idea maps directly to our multi-start approach.** We generate 10 diverse racing lines from different initializations (zero, late-apex, random). Rather than a receding-horizon re-solve as in the paper, our discrete candidate set approximates the same principle: we are searching for the locally best reference trajectory from the current operating point. The paper validates that this kind of multi-hypothesis search produces better lap times than committing to a single offline solution.

**The g-g-g-v model.** Our current trajectory optimizer uses fixed `DroneConstraints` (max velocity, max acceleration). The g-g-g-v insight is that kinematic limits are not constant — in a drone, apparent gravity changes through banked turns and aggressive pitch/roll maneuvers. While we are not on a ground vehicle, the analogous concept applies: our drone's maximum lateral acceleration budget depends on its current attitude (how much of thrust is spent countering gravity). The g-g-g-v model suggests we should parameterize constraint envelopes as a function of instantaneous state rather than using global fixed bounds.

**Cost function structure.** The six-term cost J with lateral deviation, curvature deviation, velocity deviation, and feasibility terms is a well-validated formulation. Our current proxy uses only path_length + curvature². Adding a velocity-deviation term (penalizing lines that require large deceleration due to tight corners) would make the proxy more predictive of actual tracking error, even before we implement the full kinematic sim rollout.

**Modules affected**: `planning/racing_line.py` (candidate selection criterion), `planning/trajectory_optimizer.py` (segment time allocation and constraint envelope), and potentially `control/mpc_tracker.py` (if we adopt the cost structure for the tracker as well).

## Actionable Takeaways

1. **Replace proxy selection criterion with kinematic sim rollout.** For each of the 10 L-BFGS candidate racing lines, run a fast kinematic simulation (constant-thrust point mass with drag, ~100ms wall time per candidate) and compute integrated cross-track error. Select the candidate with minimum tracking error rather than minimum `path_length + smoothness_weight * curvature²`. This directly implements the paper's insight that the correct selection metric is actual dynamic trackability.

2. **Add velocity-deviation term to the L-BFGS objective.** Before implementing full sim rollout, improve the proxy by adding a term penalizing velocity magnitude changes along the racing line (i.e., segments requiring large acceleration/deceleration). This approximates the `Δv_target` cost and will better correlate with tracking error. Concretely: compute the speed profile along each candidate using curvature-limited kinematic rollout, then penalize RMS speed deviation from the profile's mean.

3. **Implement a spatial-horizon sampling strategy for candidate diversity.** Our current random initializations produce spatially unstructured candidates. Following Langmann 2025 (the direct extension of this paper), bias the random initializations toward structurally distinct apex placements: for each gate, generate one early-apex candidate, one late-apex candidate (already done), and one minimum-curvature candidate in addition to random seeds. This gives better coverage of the candidate space.

4. **Account for 3D effects in segment time allocation.** Our `trajectory_optimizer.py` uses fixed duration estimates per segment. On vertical sections of the course (gates at different altitudes), the effective lateral acceleration budget changes because thrust is partially consumed fighting gravity (analogous to the g-g-g-v model). Parameterize segment time as a function of altitude change: vertical segments should get longer time budgets.

5. **Adopt the multi-term cost function structure for the racing line optimizer.** Replace `speed_weight * path_length + smoothness_weight * curvature²` with a weighted sum that includes: (a) path length, (b) integrated curvature², (c) integrated velocity deviation from curvature-limited speed profile, and (d) optionally a gate-center deviation penalty. This brings the proxy closer to the paper's validated cost functional.

6. **Cache the online racing line computation.** The paper's online re-solve is computationally light because it only covers a short horizon. If we implement a sim rollout for candidate selection (takeaway 1), cache the rollout result and reuse it during the race — do not re-run full optimization mid-flight. The selection happens once pre-race; the online adaptation (if needed) can be approximated by tracking the best-fitting cached candidate.

7. **Use the Frenetix open-source implementation as a reference.** The TUM-AVS Frenetix-Motion-Planner (https://github.com/TUM-AVS/Frenetix-Motion-Planner) provides a reference implementation of the cost functional and sampling strategy. While it targets ground vehicles, the cost function architecture and the C++ trajectory generator are directly applicable.

## Limitations & Caveats

**Ground vehicle vs. drone.** The entire paper is designed for a four-wheeled racing car (Dallara AV-21 / Indy Autonomous Challenge). The g-g-g-v model captures tire-road friction, which has no direct analog in multirotor flight. Our effective "friction envelope" is the thrust-to-weight ratio minus attitude coupling; the formalism is similar but the physical parameters are completely different. Do not transfer numerical values from the car domain.

**Frenet frame assumes a well-defined track centerline.** The Frenet frame decomposition requires a smooth, non-self-intersecting centerline with bounded curvature. For most drone race courses this holds, but for very tight gates or 3D helical sections, the Frenet frame can become degenerate (large curvature causes large Frenet coordinate distortion). Our gate-sequenced waypoint representation is more robust to this.

**Jerk-optimal polynomials vs. our min-snap polynomials.** The paper uses jerk-optimal (5th-order) polynomials. Our `trajectory_optimizer.py` uses min-snap (7th-order) polynomials, which are standard for multirotor systems because snap (4th derivative) is the physical input. Min-snap will produce smoother motor commands; the paper's jerk-optimal formulation is a simpler approximation that may not be directly transferable.

**Online re-solve cost.** The paper solves a truncated MLT problem online at each planning step. For a quadrotor flying at 15–20 m/s, the time budget per planning step is ~5–10ms. Implementing true online MLT re-solving at this rate requires either a very short horizon or a warm-started solver. Our current approach (pre-compute all 10 candidates offline, select the best before the race) avoids this constraint entirely.

**Multi-vehicle scenario benefits may not apply.** A major motivation for online racing line generation is handling other vehicles on track. Our race is likely solo (no opponent avoidance required), so the overtaking benefit does not apply. The main benefit for us is better adaptation to controller-induced deviations, which is more modest.

**No quantitative data available.** The paper is paywalled and the full lap-time comparison tables are not publicly accessible. We cannot directly read off which percentage improvement to expect from adopting online racing line selection vs. our current proxy objective.

## Key Parameters / Constants

- **N trajectories per planning step**: 800 trajectories in ~8ms (Frenetix C++ implementation), implying ~10,000 trajectories/second feasible on commodity hardware.
- **Cost weights (Frenetix reference implementation)**: `distance_to_reference_path = 3.0`, `lateral_jerk = 1.0`, `longitudinal_jerk = 1.0`, `velocity_offset = 0.1`, `distance_to_obstacles = 0.1`, `prediction (safety) = 200`.
- **Polynomial order**: 5th-order (jerk-optimal) for lateral, 4th-order for longitudinal in the Frenet frame. Our system uses 7th-order (min-snap) which is equivalent for trajectory smoothness purposes.
- **Spatial horizon S**: not numerically specified in publicly accessible text; Langmann 2025 describes it as "a fixed spatial horizon" (no explicit value given). For ground vehicles at 60–80 m/s, S ~ 100–200m is typical for 1–2 second look-ahead.
- **Temporal horizon T**: similarly unpublished; typical values in related TUM work are 2–4 seconds.
- **g-g-g-v parameterization**: the friction envelope is a function of velocity v (tighter at high speed due to aero downforce saturation effects) and apparent vertical acceleration az. No specific numerical values are publicly available for their car model.
- **MLT problem horizon for online racing line**: described as "limited spatial horizon" — short enough to re-solve at planning frequency (~100 Hz), implying the horizon is at most a few gates or ~50m of track.
